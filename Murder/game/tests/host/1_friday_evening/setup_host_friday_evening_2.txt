Chapter: The Arrival
Character: Lady Claythorn
The afternoon went by quickly.
I learnt which door leads where, so that I never have to ask.
I studied the names of the guests from a sheet of paper.
Most of them must have arrived by now.
I take a look in the mirror.
And I see myself dressed in elegant clothes.
The sort I am only accustomed to wearing onstage.
That should help me remember that I am playing a role here.
Someone knocks on my door.
Lady Claythorn: Come in!
Butler: I come to see that everything is ready.
Butler: Do you remember the etiquette?
Lady Claythorn: Of course I do.
I spent hours on a book about proper dinner table manners.
As if the whole plot depended on it.
Butler: Good, I have also written the speech you are to give at dinner.
Butler: Here it is.
Butler: You should know it by heart by dinner.
Butler: That would establish you without a doubt as the lady of the house.
Butler: Do it well and they will not question who you are.
Butler: Just pretend you are telling it to an audience in a theatre.
Lady Claythorn: Very well.
That, at least, I know how to do.
Butler: One more thing.
Butler: I have left a book out for you in the library.
Butler: When you have the time, you should read the part about this place.
Butler: That might be helpful.
I nod, and he leaves without ceremony.
So I start to memorise my lines.
The gong goes below.
Curtain up.
I come in last.
Seven faces turn, and I take the long walk to my chair.
I do not look at any of them until I reach my place.
I let the silence sit half a beat longer than is comfortable, and I begin.
Lady Claythorn: Welcome, everyone. My apologies for keeping you waiting.
Lady Claythorn: Now that we're all gathered, I'd like to express my gratitude once more.
Lady Claythorn: As you already know, you are here because of the heroic acts you've committed in the past.
Lady Claythorn: I've taken notice of these actions and felt it was my responsibility to extend a formal 'thank you.'
Lady Claythorn: I realise the invitation letter you received was somewhat vague, so let me clarify its contents now.
Lady Claythorn: My first gift to you is a stay at my manor.
Lady Claythorn: You'll be fully catered to, enjoying the most refined food, expertly prepared by my personal chef.
Lady Claythorn: During the three days we'll spend together, we'll also partake in various activities, which I'm sure you'll find enjoyable.
Lady Claythorn: My second gift is a sum of one thousand pounds, awarded to each and every one of you.
Lady Claythorn: This is my way of thanking those as selfless as yourselves, who help others without thought of reward.
When I am done there is that warm shuffle round a table that means it landed.
A few of them murmur their thanks.
Lady Claythorn: Please, there's no need to thank me.
Lady Claythorn: The food will be served shortly.
Lady Claythorn: Enjoy your meal.
The first course comes in on the heels of it, and the room turns into a dinner party.
My hands are shaking slightly under the cloth.
But nobody can see them.
The first act is over.
I sit down and relax for a second.
But I must start talking to the guests now.
I remember that it matters who I address first.
Menu Choice: Turn to Mr Moody, on your right
Lady Claythorn: Mr Moody.
Thomas Moody: Lady Claythorn.
Thomas Moody: I must thank you again for your generosity.
Lady Claythorn: No need, I am very happy to do it.
Lady Claythorn: But now I would like to know a little more about you.
Thomas Moody: There is not much to tell, I am afraid.
Thomas Moody: I spent my youth in service, boot boy and then footman, and after that I enlisted.
Thomas Moody: Now I live quietly on my pension in Liverpool.
Thomas Moody: But I would far rather talk about you.
Lady Claythorn: Of course, but ...
Thomas Moody: This house is splendid, for instance.
Thomas Moody: Has it been in your family long?
Lady Claythorn: Well, yes ...
He does not give me the chance to ask a single question of my own.
And that is how it goes for the whole of dinner.
I feel as though I am being interrogated, and I must be extremely careful not to give anything away.
In the end, I do not manage to ask him a single question.
The last plates go out, and it is up to me to call the end of dinner.
Lady Claythorn: It looks as though everyone has finished their meal.
Lady Claythorn: I shall let you return to your rooms, then.
Lady Claythorn: Afterwards there will be drinks in the billiard room for anyone who cares to sit up a while.
Chairs go back.
The party moves off.
And that is it for dinner.
I go up first, because I need the door shut and a moment to regroup.
But not too long.
I am expected downstairs, and failing to appear would raise questions.
But I do not need to reach the billiard room right away.
What should I do?
Map Choice: Scullery
I look at the narrow stair down.
It should lead to the servants' quarters.
A lady should not go there.
She should ring, and wait for the servants to come up to her.
But curiosity is too strong, and I go down anyway.
The scullery is empty and cold, and smells of soda and wet stone.
I take a quick look about the room.
On the shelf above the sink stands a bottle with its cork out.
Rat poison.
And it is half-empty.
In itself, it is not unusual.
Old houses have rats.
Except that this one was shut up until yesterday, and will be again at the end of this weekend.
Why would anyone bother getting rid of rats for a mere couple of days?
I leave without a good answer.
Map Choice: Kitchen
I reach the most important room below stairs, the kitchen.
The girl is there alone, sorting the pots and pans used for dinner.
I wonder how she managed to prepare an entire dinner.
She notices me.
Young woman: Ma'am, I do not think you should be here.
Lady Claythorn: I know, but I wanted to check on you.
Lady Claythorn: And how are you managing so far?
Young woman: Everything went well, ma'am. We all pitched in for dinner.
Young woman: And we will probably spend the night clearing it all up, but we will manage.
Lady Claythorn: Very well, I will not disturb you then.
Lady Claythorn: Good night.
Young woman: Good night, m'lady.
Map Choice: Garage
Petrol and cold iron, and a different car from the one we came up in.
An old tourer, well kept.
It does not look as though it will start.
Nothing for me here.
Map Choice: Gun room
Sporting guns behind glass, and a handgun lying out on the table.
It reminds me of a theatre trope I once read about.
Chekhov's Gun.
Better leave it there.
Map Choice: Storage Room
I climb the stairs, even though I probably should not.
I try to open the door.
Locked, of course.
And I do not have the key.
Only he does.
Out of curiosity, I try another door, then a third, then the last.
All of them closed.
I stand on the stair a moment longer than I need to, and then I go back down.
Map Choice: William the Conqueror Bedroom
I have my hand on the door before the sense of it catches up with me.
No.
There is nothing in that room worth what it would cost me.
A guest found on the wrong landing is an eccentric. The lady of the house coming out of a guest's bedroom is a story that reaches the village by Monday.
And if there is one thing this house cannot afford, it is a story it did not write itself.
Map Choice: Tea room
The tea room has been put straight already.
Cushions plumped, glasses gone, not a ring left on the wood.
I am exhausted, so I head back to my room for the night.
Someone knocking at this hour.
That can only be him.
Butler: My lady?
Lady Claythorn: Yes, come on in.
He enters and immediately loses his air of obedience.
Butler: Well, the first day is done, but I am afraid it was not without mistakes.
Lady Claythorn: Really, what happened?
Butler: You went to your right first at dinner.
Butler: You should have gone to your left, as was plainly set out in the book of rules I gave you.
Lady Claythorn: Oh, sorry, I forgot about that rule.
Lady Claythorn: But surely nobody noticed.
Butler: Maybe, but I am not sure.
Butler: I think some of them might be more accustomed to that sort of thing than we first thought.
Butler: That is not the whole of it.
Butler: You went below stairs.
Butler: The mistress of a house does not go down to the servants' floor.
Butler: She rings, and she waits, and they come up to her.
Lady Claythorn: Surely that cannot be so bad.
Lady Claythorn: I am sure nobody noticed.
Butler: You cannot be certain of that.
Butler: Even so, you might have run by chance into a guest, and that would have raised questions.
Butler: You should not take such a risk.
Lady Claythorn: Very well.
Butler: And the last of it.
Butler: You invited them for drinks and then did not appear in the billiard room at all.
Butler: They sat up waiting for their hostess to look in.
Butler: No doubt questions were raised.
Butler: None of it is fatal on its own.
Butler: But taken all together, it might attract attention.
Butler: You should do better tomorrow.
He is right, of course.
I have not been careful enough.
If I want this weekend to be a success, I should pay more attention to details.
Butler: Now, if you will excuse me.
Butler: We shall meet again tomorrow.
Lady Claythorn: Of course, good night.
He leaves without another word.
I suppose he will only play the dutiful servant when we have company.
Anyway, it has been a long day, so I take my clothes off and get into bed.
It is not long before I fall asleep.
