Chapter: The Hunt
Character: Lady Claythorn

But I do not have much time, so I get ready without thinking too much about it.
Soon, I am ready to join the others for the hunt.
First I must take a weapon.

Lady Claythorn: That is true.
True, but I may have exaggerated my abilities in order to get this job.
I once went on a shoot with a fellow who was courting me.
I hope that will be enough.

Butler: If you are not sure...

Butler: All you have to do is not embarrass yourself.
Lady Claythorn: Do not worry, I will be fine.

Butler: Good, now that everyone is ready, I propose we split into two groups.
Butler: It will be easier to spot game that way.

Lady Claythorn: How gallant of you, Captain. The privilege, I assure you, is mine.

The Captain, of all of them.

The butler, who has been listening with the unobtrusive attention of his trade, steps forward.

Butler: My lady and Captain Sinha to the north field, and I shall attend.

I ought to be grateful for that, and I find that I am.
We walk for some time through the undergrowth, and I remember that a gun is a great deal heavier than it looks.
I shift my grip and find no comfortable way to hold the thing.
A pheasant goes up out of the grass with a noise like a slammed door.
Instinctively, I raise my rifle.
The shot comes up late, and too high.
The bird flies off into the trees.
The gun kicks back into my shoulder hard enough to bring water to my eyes.
I am not off to a great start.
Lady Claythorn: The light is most unhelpful today.
Nobody answers me.
The light is perfectly fine, and they know it.
I ought to have said nothing at all.
Butler: It is true, Captain, as her ladyship says.
Butler: I have known mornings here when a man could not hit a barn door at ten paces.
He smiles at me and nods.
But I am certain he noticed how poor my shot was.
A while later, the Captain misses a shot of his own.
I am starting to think he is not a natural hunter either.
Or maybe he is a little rusty.
Then, a little further on, I notice something behind a bush.
A rabbit, some twenty paces off.
The Captain has not seen it. Nor has the butler.
For the moment it belongs to me alone.
It is sitting perfectly still, and surely even I cannot miss this.
I could fire the gun.
Or I could just let the Captain take the shot.

I have fired once this morning, and once was quite enough for everybody.

Lady Claythorn: I have the whole season for such things, and you have come a very long way.
Sushil Sinha: You are very good, my lady.

He misses.

A creature sitting perfectly still at twenty paces, and an officer of thirty years' service cannot touch it.
Lady Claythorn: Captain, that was a nice attempt.

Sushil Sinha: You are too kind, my lady.

The light.
He has taken my weak excuse and put it straight into his own mouth.
I have spent twenty years among people pretending to be something they are not, and I know the sound of it.
Captain Sinha cannot shoot.
Maybe I am not the only one wearing a costume this morning.
Nothing else shows itself, which is the first piece of luck I have had all morning.
By the time the butler calls us in for luncheon, I have not been obliged to lift the gun again.
The butler lays out a modest spread on a linen cloth, pours tea, then steps back with a brief bow.
Butler: If you don't mind, my lady. I should like to look in upon the other party, that they might not want for anything.
Butler: I shall be back soon.
Lady Claythorn: Very well. Thank you.
So I am left alone with the Captain, and an hour of it to fill.
We talk of the weather, and of the birches, and of how much colder it is here than in London.
He asks nothing about the house, and nothing about my family, which is more than I dared hope for.
Whatever he thinks of me, he is too well mannered to say it over cold chicken.
In due course the butler returns, composed as ever, and we resume the hunt.
Two shots, close together, from the direction of the western grove.
Lady Claythorn: It seems the others have seen something.
Then, a moment later, a different sound.
A cry. Thin and urgent, carried between the trees.
The Captain is already running before I have made sense of what I have heard.
I gather my skirts and go after him.

Mr Harring kneels beside him, white as paper. Samuel Manning stands a few paces off, trembling, his rifle in the moss at his feet.
I played a scene very like this one years ago, in a theatre in Sheffield.
I remember thinking the blood was overdone.
Samuel Manning: A rabbit. It was a rabbit.

Lady Claythorn: Good God, what has happened?

An accident, then.
That is the second one in a single day, and I am to stand here and believe both of them.
Nobody is doing anything for the doctor, and that is how I know he is already gone.
I step back from him, and back again, until a sapling stops me.
Lady Claythorn: He is... he is truly dead?
Lady Claythorn: But this cannot be. This was meant to be...

A pleasant weekend. That is what it was meant to be.
I turn my face away from the doctor and remember, far too late, who I am supposed to be.
Lady Claythorn: Forgive me. I am being of no use whatsoever.
Lady Claythorn: Captain, you must... you must tell us what is to be done.

Sushil Sinha: The doctor's bag will hold a blade.

His hands know the work without being told, and I find myself wondering how many times he has done it before.

Sushil Sinha: Mr Harring at the head, the footman at the feet.
Sushil Sinha: Walk in step. Short paces.

Sushil Sinha: Mr Manning, follow behind with his bag.
We begin the slow march back through the ferns.
The butler walks a few paces behind me on the way back, and says nothing at all.
He was away the whole hour, and he came back to us only minutes before those shots were fired.
Two deaths in one day, and I am the mistress of the place where both of them happened.
Whatever this weekend was arranged for, it was not this.
